# Thinking in Systems: The Tub Model

**Find the tub before you blame the hand.** When something keeps happening, picture a bathtub with someone watching a gauge and turning the faucet. Then ask four questions before you change anything:

1. **What's piling up?** This is the stock: a backlog, trust, dirty dishes, savings.
2. **Who sees the level, and how?** This is the gauge.
3. **What are they steering toward?** This is the goal, judged by how they act, not by what anyone says.
4. **How late does the news reach them?** This is the delay.

The decider is whoever acts on what the gauge shows, and the faucet is whatever they change.

> **Source:** Distilled from *Thinking in Systems: A Primer* by Donella H. Meadows, by way of the [Mastery Guide](Thinking-in-Systems-Mastery-Guide.md) and [Study Map](Thinking-in-Systems-Study-Map.md) in this folder. The picture and the mapping of the book's ideas onto it are Claude's synthesis, written Nov 18, 2026, not the book's own framing.

```
                    GOAL
            what they steer toward
                     │
                     ▼
   GAUGE ───────► DECIDER ───────► FAUCET
   what they      who acts         what they
   can see                         change
     ▲                                │
     │                                ▼
     └──── DELAY ──── [ THE TUB ] ◄───┘
                   what's piling up
```

This picture complements the events, behavior, and structure pyramid in the Mastery Guide. The pyramid tells you to look deeper but doesn't say what to look for, and this picture does. It's a single feedback loop that steers a level toward a goal. Many of the book's traps are that loop with one part broken or rewired.

## Most of the book's traps break one part of the picture

| What's broken | What you see | Book's name |
|---|---|---|
| The gauge reads nobody's tub, or someone else's | Each user gets the full gain, and the shared cost barely reaches them | Tragedy of the commons |
| The goal slides toward whatever the gauge shows | "We're not much worse than last year" | Drift to low performance |
| The goal is the gauge's number, not the real thing | Measure test scores and you get test scores, whether or not students learn more | Seeking the wrong goal |
| The goal is set by a rival's tub | Price wars, arms races | Escalation |
| Two deciders with different goals share one tub | Huge effort and nothing moves | Policy resistance |
| A long delay between faucet and gauge | Overshoot, then undershoot | Not a named trap: the car dealer's delays |
| Someone outside turns the faucet for you | The symptom eases while your own capacity withers | Shifting the burden |
| The fuller the tub, the wider its faucet opens | Winners get the means to win again | Success to the successful |

## The power to change a system sits higher in the picture

Meadows ranks 12 places to intervene, and the faucet settings (the numbers) and the size and plumbing of the tub sit at the bottom. They get nearly all the attention and have the least power. Delays and the strength of the loops rank higher, and the gauge ranks higher still. Moving the Dutch electric meters into the front hall cut use by about a third, with no change in price.

Above the gauge come the rules about who may turn the faucet, then the goal, and at the top the belief that set the goal. When you feel the urge to turn the faucet harder, move up one step instead.

## Two examples

**At home.** Picture dishes in a shared sink. The person who adds a dish never feels the pile grow, so the only gauge that works belongs to whoever cares most. Nagging is just turning the faucet. A rule that you rinse your own dish before leaving the kitchen puts the cost on the person who made it, right away.

**At work.** Picture provider messaging quality. The tub is patient trust, which fills and drains one message at a time. If the gauge a provider sees is queue size and handle time, they will steer toward speed, whatever the charter says. Swapping in different providers rarely changes that.

If retention moves well after the messages that affect it, the delay is the hard part. A tub with a long delay needs a gauge that reads early, which is a job the Quality of Care measure could do. Whether anyone steers by that gauge then depends on the rules, such as how providers are paid.
